The beginnings

A farmhouse in the hills of Pisa

In 1536 the plain of Pisa was still largely marshland and, in some seasons, under water. To settle the hills of Monte Pisano, poor labouring families were called in: they received a house and a plot of land in exchange for their work and a share of the harvest.

That is how Belvedere came to be: a stone farmhouse with a stable, a threshing floor and the typical dovecote tower, built where the view opens onto the valley – hence its name, “beautiful view”. The farm was part of the estates of the Counts Agostini Fantini Venerosi della Seta, a noble family of Pisa. At that time the hill was still covered in forest.

Timeline

Five centuries at a glance

  1. 1536

    The settlement

    Sharecropping families settle on the slopes of Monte Pisano. The Belvedere farmhouse is built as part of the estates of the Counts Agostini Fantini Venerosi della Seta.

  2. c. 1735

    From forest to olive grove

    The mezzadri begin clearing the forest and planting olive trees, most of them supported by small crescent-shaped dry-stone walls, the “lunette”. Some of those trees still grow today.

  3. 18th–20th c.

    The age of sharecropping

    The harvest is split in half between landowner and farming family. Up to three generations live in the house, knowing every tree by name.

  4. 1964

    The end of an era

    The lands of Count Agostini Fantini Venerosi della Seta pass to a Sardinian building contractor – except for Villa di Corliano at the foot of Monte Pisano, directly below Belvedere. The mezzadri leave the Villa.

  5. 1964 – 1987

    Uncertain years

    Years of unclear ownership follow, during which the olive grove is no longer tended as it once was.

  6. 1987

    Rebirth

    Munich entrepreneur Rolf Freitag buys the Villa and renovates it completely over several years. The olive grove, too, is turned back into a productive grove.

  7. 2024

    A new chapter

    Ralph and Christina Striewski buy the Villa and the olive grove, together with the Azienda Agricola Villa Belvedere di Corliano, its organic certification and its own olive mill.

The lunette: olive trees won from the forest

Around 1735 the mezzadri began to transform the hill. They cleared the forest by hand, pulled the stones from the soil and used those very stones to build a small crescent-shaped wall around each young olive tree: the lunetta (half-moon). It holds soil and rainwater on the steep slope and protects the roots.

Most of our olive trees still stand inside their lunetta today. Some are around 300 years old: those sharecroppers planted them, thinking of their grandchildren.

The day followed the sun. Trees were pruned in spring, the soil hoed in summer, and in autumn the harvest came – long poles beating the branches above cloths spread on the ground. The women looked after the garden, the animals and the house; the children gathered fallen olives, one by one. Half the harvest belonged to the Count.

From the Count’s mill to our own mill

For centuries Belvedere’s olives went down to the valley in baskets, to the Count’s mill at Corliano: a large stone room where a granite millstone crushed the fruit into a paste that was then pressed on mats. The oil rested in terracotta jars; part of it went to the Count, the rest stayed with the farm family – all year round it was food, medicine and light.

In 1964 sharecropping ended and the mezzadri left the Villa. After years of unclear ownership, Rolf Freitag bought the estate in 1987, restored it carefully and brought the olive grove back into production. In 2024 we took over from him not only the Villa and the grove, but also the Azienda Agricola Villa Belvedere di Corliano, with its organic certification and its own olive mill.

That is why today the whole process – from harvest to bottle – takes place right here on our estate.

At the foot of Monte Pisano

Villa di Corliano

Directly below Belvedere, at the foot of Monte Pisano, stands Villa di Corliano, seat of the Counts Agostini Fantini Venerosi della Seta. In 1964 it was the only part of their estates that did not pass to the Sardinian building contractor.

The villa also includes the Count’s old olive mill, where the olives of the hill – Belvedere’s among them – were pressed for centuries. It can still be visited today.
