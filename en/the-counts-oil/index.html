The beginnings

A farmhouse in the hills of Pisa

In 1536 the plain of Pisa was still largely marshland and, in some seasons, under water. To bring the hills of Monte Pisano under cultivation, poor labouring families were called in: they received a house and a plot of land in exchange for their work and a share of the harvest.

That is how Belvedere came to be: a stone farmhouse with a stable, a threshing floor and the typical dovecote tower, built where the view opens onto the valley – hence its name, “beautiful view”. The Count’s mill and palazzo

Belvedere’s olives were not pressed at home. They went down to the valley in baskets, to the Count’s mill: a large, dark and warm stone room where a granite millstone – turned first by an animal, later by water – crushed the fruit into paste. The paste was spread on mats and pressed; the oil rested in terracotta jars.

Part of the oil made its way to the town palazzo in Pisa, where it lit the lamps, dressed the Count’s table and was traded for other goods. The rest stayed with the farm family: all year round it was food, medicine and light.

The life of the sharecroppers

The day followed the sun. Trees were pruned in spring, the soil hoed in summer, and in autumn the harvest came – long poles beating the branches above cloths spread on the ground. The women looked after the vegetable garden, the animals and the house; the children gathered fallen olives, one by one.

The sharecropping contract bound family and farm for generations. It was a hard bargain, but it shaped the landscape we still see today: dry-stone walls, terraces, paths and olive trees planted by someone thinking of their grandchildren.
